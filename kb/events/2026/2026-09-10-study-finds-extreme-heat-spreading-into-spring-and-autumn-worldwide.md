A study published in AGU Advances and summarised by its lead author, a postdoctoral researcher at New York University, analyses whether the calendar timing of extreme heat events is shifting worldwide[^carbonbrief-heat-shoulder-seasons]. Using MERRA2 reanalysis data from 1980-2024, cross-checked with ERA5, the researchers compared the 1980s baseline decade to 2015-2024 and found that in the 1980s extreme heat was tightly confined to defined 'local heat seasons', with 93% of land area seeing over 80% of extreme dry-heat days within that season[^carbonbrief-heat-shoulder-seasons]. By 2015-2024, extreme heat events had spread into two-month 'shoulder seasons' before or after the traditional heat season across more than half of global land area, with western Europe, southern Africa and north-western India seeing more heat creep into spring, while the US, eastern China, northern Africa and eastern Europe saw more creep into autumn[^carbonbrief-heat-shoulder-seasons]. The authors tested whether simple uniform annual warming could explain this and found it accounts for shifts within the traditional season but not the uneven shoulder-season spread, suggesting factors like changing precipitation, soil moisture and land-use patterns, or natural climate oscillations, are also involved[^carbonbrief-heat-shoulder-seasons].
