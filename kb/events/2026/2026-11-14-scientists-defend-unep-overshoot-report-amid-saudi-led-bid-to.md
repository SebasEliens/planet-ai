Authors of UNEP's 'Limiting Overshoot' report, published in September, defended its credibility at COP31 after the Saudi-led Arab Group and the Like-Minded Developing Countries bloc, including India and China, tried to keep it out of the summit's cover decision by questioning whether it had been properly peer-reviewed[^climatechangenews-2026-11-14]. Co-author Debra Roberts, former IPCC Working Group II co-chair, said the report followed standard global-assessment practice with internal and external peer review by 44 reviewers of work from 44 authors, differing from IPCC reports only in lacking formal government review[^climatechangenews-2026-11-14]. Climate Analytics CEO Bill Hare argued the pushback stemmed from governments reliant on fossil fuel revenues not wanting to accept responsibility for overshoot-related damages, while European Climate Foundation's Laurence Tubiana said the report "tells us a truth some would rather hide"[^climatechangenews-2026-11-14]. The dispute reflects a broader fight over whether 'grey literature' such as UNEP assessments can inform COP political texts, with LDC advisor Manjeet Dhakal defending the report's use to guide negotiators in Antalya[^climatechangenews-2026-11-14].
