On October 21, 2026, EFF published a detailed state legislative agenda aimed at ending police use of Automated License Plate Readers (ALPRs) for mass surveillance[^eff-alpr-agenda-2026]. The post argues that AI-driven advances and nationwide data sharing have turned ALPR networks—marketed by vendors like Flock Safety, Motorola Solutions, and Axon—into dragnet tracking systems that have been used to search for immigrants, monitor protesters, target Romani communities, and track people seeking reproductive healthcare[^eff-alpr-agenda-2026]. EFF notes that in 2026 six states (Connecticut, Kentucky, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon, and Washington) passed laws limiting police ALPR use, and proposes a tiered model for other states ranging from outright bans to strict limits on hotlists, data storage, predictive policing uses, and sensitive-location placement[^eff-alpr-agenda-2026]. The framework also calls for transparency measures such as a 30-day public notice before ALPR procurement, a statewide registry, regular audits, and enforcement mechanisms including a private right of action[^eff-alpr-agenda-2026].
