On October 21, 2026, EFF published a detailed state legislative agenda aimed at curbing or eliminating police use of Automated License Plate Readers (ALPRs), framing the AI-driven, interconnected camera networks as mass surveillance infrastructure rather than a simple crime-fighting tool[^eff-alpr-2026]. The post documents EFF's prior findings that ALPR data has been used to track immigrants, protesters, Romani communities, and people seeking reproductive healthcare, and notes officers logging frivolous search justifications like "LOL" and "LMAO"[^eff-alpr-2026]. It reports that in 2026 six U.S. states—Connecticut, Kentucky, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon, and Washington—enacted new restrictions on police ALPR use, building on New Hampshire's first-in-the-nation 2007 law[^eff-alpr-2026]. EFF's proposed model goes further, calling for outright statewide bans where possible, and otherwise urging warrant requirements for watchlists, strict data storage/search/disclosure limits, bans on predictive policing uses and ALPR placement near sensitive locations, mandatory transparency registries and audits, and enforceable penalties such as a private right of action[^eff-alpr-2026].
