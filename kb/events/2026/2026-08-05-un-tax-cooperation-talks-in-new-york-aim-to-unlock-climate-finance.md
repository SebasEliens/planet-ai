Governments gathered in New York this week for negotiations under the UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation, aiming to replace the current system that lets multinationals report profits wherever they choose with a unitary taxation model based on real economic activity[^climatechangenews-2026-08-05]. Tax Justice Network staff writing about the talks estimate the reform could generate around $500 billion annually in extra corporate tax revenue, close to 40% of the $1.3 trillion climate finance goal governments set for 2035[^climatechangenews-2026-08-05]. They note that while wealthier countries like France would gain the most in absolute terms (an estimated $25.5 billion more per year), lower-income countries such as Kenya could see proportional revenue increases as high as 406%[^climatechangenews-2026-08-05]. The authors frame the current 'arm's length' tax rule as a legal fiction that lets profit be shifted away from where economic activity actually occurs, undermining public revenue needed for climate resilience and clean energy investment[^climatechangenews-2026-08-05].
